It happened in a walk-in aviary, the kind with tall mesh walls, warm light and a lot of happy noise. Birds were everywhere: on branches, on feeders, on railings, on other people's shoulders. I held out my hand, mostly as a joke.

A small parrot took it seriously.

It landed, looked me over, hopped up my arm and settled on my shoulder as if it had booked the seat in advance. Bright yellow, green on the wings, a splash of orange around the face, like someone had painted it in a hurry and then decided it was perfect.

I froze. Not out of fear. I just didn't want to be the reason it left.

## Being chosen

There's a strange feeling when a small creature picks you. You can hold out your hand, but you can't make anything land on it. It looks at a crowd of people and, for reasons only it knows, decides you'll do.

I won't pretend it was deep wisdom on the bird's part. It probably liked the stripes on my shirt. Still, it felt like a small honour, the kind you can't earn and can't ask for. You can only try to deserve it while it lasts. Maybe that's why the moment felt so big. Nothing about it was planned.

## The trick is to be still

A few more birds soon found my arm, and I learned something quickly. They didn't respond to what I wanted. They responded to how I moved.

When I moved fast, they shuffled and fluttered. When I slowed down, they settled. When I stopped trying to get the perfect photo and just stood there, they stayed.

> When I stopped trying to get the perfect photo and just stood there, they stayed.

I think about that outside the aviary too. Some good things only come close when you stop chasing them: ideas, friendships, quiet moments. You make yourself calm, kind and a little bit patient, and sometimes something beautiful decides to sit with you for a while.

## Small creatures, big trust

A bird that fits in your palm is trusting someone many times its size. It has every reason to be careful. When it lands on you anyway, it's saying something simple: I think you're gentle.

That's a lot of responsibility for one man in a striped shirt.

So I tried to deserve it. Slow hands. Soft voice. No sudden turns. When it was ready, it hopped off to a branch without any fuss, as if this were all perfectly ordinary. For the bird, it probably was. For me, it was anything but.

## The same is true online

I didn't expect a parrot to teach me anything about making content, but it did. When you share your work, it's tempting to be loud, fast and everywhere, hoping attention lands on you. It rarely works that way. People come closer to work that feels calm, honest and consistent, the same way the birds came closer once I stopped flapping around myself.

So I try to post the way I stood in that aviary. Steady. Open. Not grabbing. If something you make is gentle and real, the right people tend to find it, and stay.

## What I took home

Be still more often. Be gentle with small things: animals, children, nervous people, and your own ideas when they're new and easily scared away. Trust usually arrives in small amounts, and it's precious every time.

And wear stripes. You never know who's looking for a place to land.

### Little things I noticed

- Its feet were warm, and much lighter than I expected.
- Parrots tilt their heads to look at you properly, one eye at a time.
- Yellow looks even brighter next to black-and-white stripes.
- Standing still is harder than it looks. Try it with a parrot next to your ear.
